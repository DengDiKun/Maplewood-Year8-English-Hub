Unit 8.1

The Merchant of Venice

Main classroom focus: Activities 4 & 5.

Open Unit 8.1 →

How to teach it

  1. Begin with the choice itself. Show or name the three caskets: gold, silver and lead. Ask students which they would choose before discussing the riddles. Do not correct them yet.
  2. Read dramatically. Give students the roles of Portia, Morocco, Aragon and Bassanio. Pause after each choice and ask: “What does this choice tell us about him?”
  3. Model inference before Activity 4. Take one short quotation and think aloud: identify the words, infer the attitude, then connect it to the moral lesson.
  4. Complete Activity 4 as an evidence table. Students should separate attitude from moral lesson. Ask them to justify each point with wording from the extract.
  5. Turn Activity 5 into evidence stations. Give the three views one at a time. Students decide Agree / Partly agree / Disagree, then find evidence before speaking.
  6. Finish with interpretation. Ask students what Shakespeare may be saying about appearance, pride, love and choice.

What to emphasise

  • Attitude is not the same as action. Students should explain what the character’s choice reveals about how he thinks.
  • Morocco: connect gold, desire and outward value. Emphasise the lesson behind “All that glitters is not gold”.
  • Aragon: focus on “I will assume desert” and how confidence in what he “deserves” suggests pride.
  • Bassanio: focus on hesitation, risk and his willingness to look beyond appearance.
  • Evidence must support the view. A quotation should not be dropped into an answer without explanation.
  • Activity 5 is interpretive. More than one view can be defended if students use relevant evidence.
Teacher language: “Which word gives you that idea?” → “What does it suggest?” → “How does that support the view?”

Watch for these misconceptions

  • Students retelling which casket was chosen instead of analysing the character’s attitude.
  • Students saying “gold is bad” rather than explaining the moral about appearance and true value.
  • Students treating every Activity 5 statement as definitely true or false.
  • Students quoting a line but not explaining its meaning.

Quick success check

Before moving on, ask students to answer orally:

Morocco
What does his choice reveal about what he values?
Aragon
How does pride influence his judgement?
Bassanio
Why is his attitude different from the other two?
Unit 8.2

Life choices

Main classroom focus: Activities 2 & 3.

Open Unit 8.2 →

How to teach it

  1. Use the pre-learning video as the hook. Ask students for one choice they have made recently and what influenced it.
  2. Visualise the metaphor. Draw a forked road. Label each road with a possible life choice so students understand how a physical road can represent a decision.
  3. Read the poem aloud twice. First for general meaning; second time, students underline words showing hesitation, uncertainty or reflection.
  4. Model Activity 2. Use the pattern: quotation → feeling → explanation. Demonstrate one line, then let students complete the remaining quotations.
  5. Read ‘No Going Back’ as a contrast text. Pause at “looked back hesitantly” and the final sentence. Ask how Oliver’s physical action can also represent growing up.
  6. Build Activity 3 gradually. Complete the comparison notes first. Only after the table is secure should students write the two comparison paragraphs.

What to emphasise

  • Implicit meaning: students must explain what a line suggests, not only paraphrase it.
  • Extended metaphor: the roads can represent life choices; the diving board/dive can represent risk, commitment and growing up.
  • Comparison language: use “similarly”, “however”, “whereas”, “in contrast” to connect the two texts.
  • Use of evidence: each comparison paragraph should contain relevant textual support from both texts where possible.
  • Adverbs: words such as “steadily”, “hesitantly” and “almost” reveal manner, degree and attitude.
  • Structure: do not focus only on language. Students should notice poem form/rhythm and the prose ending “There was no going back.”
Teacher language: “What happens?” is retrieval. “What does this suggest about the character?” is analysis. Keep pushing students towards the second question.

Watch for these misconceptions

  • Students saying the poem is literally only about walking in a forest.
  • Students writing one paragraph about the poem and another about the story without actually comparing them.
  • Students identifying an adverb but not explaining its effect.
  • Students using quotations that do not support the comparison point.

Quick success check

A secure student should be able to complete this frame:

Both texts present choice as ________. In the poem, “________” suggests ________. Similarly / However, Oliver “________”, which shows ________.

Listen for a clear comparison, evidence from both texts and an explanation of effect.

Unit 8.3

Making decisions

Main classroom focus: Activities 2–5.

Open Unit 8.3 →

How to teach it

  1. Activate the theme. Use the pre-learning task, then ask students what makes a decision “good”: the outcome, the process, or both?
  2. Run Activity 2 with clear roles. Use chairperson, prompt person, evidence checker and summariser. Require each group to give one advantage and one disadvantage before choosing a suggestion.
  3. Model skimming and scanning. For the article, first skim each paragraph for the gist. Then scan for names, experiment details, results and examples.
  4. Teach main vs subsidiary information explicitly. Think aloud with one paragraph: “If I could keep only one sentence, what must stay?” That is the main point. The details that explain or support it are subsidiary.
  5. For Activity 4, give each paragraph a job label. For example: introduces idea → explains experiment → reports results → gives implications. Then examine the linking/time adverbs.
  6. For Activity 5, plan before writing. Students should note the available choices, decision process and feelings afterwards, then organise the account using linking/time adverbs.

What to emphasise

  • Main point ≠ first sentence. It is the central idea of the whole paragraph.
  • Subsidiary information includes examples, names, procedures, statistics/details and explanations that support the main point.
  • Paragraph function: students should describe the job a paragraph performs in the whole article, not simply repeat its content.
  • Linking adverbs show sequence or connection: “First”, “Next”, “Afterwards”.
  • Time adverbs tell when something happens: “Last year”, “Recently”, “Soon”.
  • Account writing: the writing must address all three bullet points and use sequencing naturally rather than inserting adverbs mechanically.
  • 150 words: encourage concise detail and a clear beginning → decision process → result/reflection structure.
Teacher language: Ask, “Is this the big idea, or is it a detail that supports the big idea?” Repeat this distinction throughout Activity 3.

Watch for these misconceptions

  • Students copying whole sentences as “main points”.
  • Students putting researcher names and experiment details in the main-point column.
  • Students describing paragraph content instead of its structural function.
  • Students confusing linking adverbs with time adverbs.
  • Students writing a story in Activity 5 without explaining the decision-making process.

Quick success check

Reading
Can the student reduce each article paragraph to one clear main point?
Language
Can the student identify an adverb and explain what connection/time relationship it creates?
Writing
Does the account cover choices, process and feelings, in a clear sequence?

Suggested lesson emphasis order

1. Theme of decision-making2. Main vs subsidiary information3. Paragraph function4. Linking/time adverbs5. Account structure

If time is limited, prioritise the distinction between main and subsidiary information and the use of linking/time adverbs, because these directly support Activities 3, 4 and 5.
