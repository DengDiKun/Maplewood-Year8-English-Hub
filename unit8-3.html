A choice problem

You are now going to read an article by Kate Douglas and Dan Jones that analyses decision-making. It describes research by scientists and suggests an unusual way to reach a decision.

Top 10 ways to make better decisions

We tend to believe that we will always be happier being in control than having someone else choose for us. Yet sometimes, no matter what the outcome of a decision, the actual process of making it can leave us feeling dissatisfied. Then it may be better to relinquish control.

Last year, Simona Botti from Cornell University and Ann McGill from the University of Chicago published a series of experiments that explore this idea. They asked volunteers to randomly select one item from a list of four. They later realised that two items were ones with pleasant smells (such as coffee or chocolate), and two were items with unpleasant smells. Once the choice was made they completed questionnaires to rate their levels of satisfaction with the outcome and to indicate how they felt about making the decision.

As you might expect, people given a choice of pleasant options tended to be very satisfied with the item they picked and happily took the credit for making a good decision. When the choice was between nasty options, though, dissatisfaction was rife: people did not like their choice, and what’s more, they tended to blame themselves for ending up with something distasteful. They would have been happier not to choose at all.

Botti believes these findings have broad implications for any decision. Try letting someone else choose the restaurant, for example. You might also feel happier about leaving some decisions to a professional. Botti’s latest work suggests that people prefer having a doctor make choices about which treatment they should have. ‘There is a fixation with choice, a belief that it brings happiness,’ she says. ‘Sometimes it doesn’t.’

relinquish: to give up something
rife: very common
implications: conclusions that are not explicitly stated
fixation: an obsession

Language focus

Key words

linking adverb: an adverb that shows a sequence in time or a contrast (for example, ‘afterwards’)

time adverb: an adverb that shows when something happens (for example, ‘yesterday’)

In writing that describes a sequence of events, gives contrasts or makes references to time, you can use specific adverbs to help the reader see connections.

The words underlined in the example here are linking adverbs. These help the reader see the stages in a scientist’s career.

  • It takes a long time to become a scientist. First, I had to study hard at school. Next, I went to university. Afterwards, I looked for a job near my home town.

The words underlined in the next example are time adverbs. These show the process of the scientist’s work and allow the reader to see how stages link together over time.

  • Last year, I decided to start some new research. Recently, I made a breakthrough. Soon, I will share my findings.

Post-submission learning guide

How to answer + worked examples

Compare your approach with the method below. These are examples of how to construct an answer, not the answers to the article.

Activity 2 — Discussion: How to answer
Use the pattern: opinion → advantage → disadvantage → response to another view → final reasoned decision. There is no single correct choice.
Example: “School uniforms should be optional.” A balanced response might explain one benefit, one problem, then give a final position with a reason.
Activity 3 — Main vs subsidiary: How to answer
Write the paragraph’s big idea in one short sentence. Then list only details that support it. Names, examples, methods and results are usually subsidiary information.
Example: In a paragraph about a recycling project, “The school launched a recycling programme” could be the main point, while the launch date, number of bins and student participation are supporting details.
Activity 3 — Checking your paragraph 2 notes
Ask: “If I removed all the details, what single idea would remain?” That idea is probably your main point. Then check whether each subsidiary detail clearly supports it.
Example: Main point: Researchers tested a new study method. Details: who took part, how long the test lasted and what participants had to do.
Activity 3 — Checking your paragraph 3 notes
When a paragraph gives results, the main point should summarise the overall finding. Do not copy every result into the main-point box.
Example: Main point: Students remembered more after using diagrams. Details: the percentage increase and which group improved most.
Activity 3 — Checking your paragraph 4 notes
For a concluding paragraph, look for wider meaning, recommendation, implication or application. Supporting examples belong in subsidiary information.
Example: Main point: The research could change how schools teach revision. Detail: the writer suggests using more visual revision tools.
Activity 4a — Paragraph functions
Start each answer with a precise job verb: introduces, explains, describes, reports, contrasts, applies or concludes. Then add what information is being handled.
Example: “Paragraph 2 explains how the survey was carried out.” This is stronger than simply retelling the survey details.
Activity 4b — Linking & time adverbs
Use three steps: identify the word → state the relationship → explain the effect on the reader.
Example: In “First, the class voted. Afterwards, they discussed the result,” the adverbs show sequence and make the stages easy to follow.
Activity 5 — Account writing
Use a simple three-part shape: choices available → decision process → feelings afterwards. Add time/linking adverbs so the order is clear.
Useful starters: At first… / First… / Next… / However… / Afterwards… / Eventually… / Looking back…
Worked account example — different situation:

Last term, I had to decide whether to join the art club or continue football training because both activities took place on the same afternoon. At first, I wanted to choose football because I had already trained with the team for several months. However, I was also interested in learning digital drawing. First, I spoke to both teachers and asked about future competitions. Next, I considered which activity I would regret missing more. Afterwards, I decided to continue football and attend short art workshops during the holidays instead. I felt slightly disappointed because I could not do both activities every week. Eventually, however, I felt satisfied with my decision because I had considered my options carefully. Looking back, I learned that making a good decision sometimes means accepting that every choice has a disadvantage.

Notice: the example answers all three bullet points and uses time/linking adverbs to organise the sequence.
