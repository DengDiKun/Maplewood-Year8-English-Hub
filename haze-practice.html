The Day the Car Park Disappeared

At seven-thirty on Tuesday morning, Aldarico stepped out of his father’s car and stared at the school entrance. Usually, he could see the basketball court, the guardhouse and half the secondary block from the drop-off area. That morning, however, the school gates seemed to float inside a grey soup.

The sun looked like a tired orange coin behind the haze. Students walked towards the building slowly, appearing and disappearing as the smoke swallowed the car park. Aldarico tightened his mask and followed the faint outline of someone ahead.

“Yu Chen?” he called.

The figure turned around.

It was Yong En.

“You seriously can’t recognise me?” she asked.

“Today, I can barely recognise the school,” Aldarico replied.

Yu Chen eventually appeared through the haze and laughed at them both.

Inside the building, the air smelled cleaner, but everyone was still talking about the strange morning. Some students joked that the haze had finally given them permission to arrive late because they “couldn’t find the school”.

Aldarico laughed too. Then he looked through the classroom window.

The buildings opposite had completely vanished.

For the first time, the joke did not seem quite so funny.

When Smoke Crosses Borders

When Smoke Crosses Borders

Haze may appear to be only a local problem, but air does not stop at national borders. During periods of dry weather, smoke from large fires can travel hundreds of kilometres with the wind. As a result, neighbouring countries may experience poor visibility and unhealthy air even though the fires themselves are far away.

Some fires begin when land is cleared for agriculture. Fires in peatland can be particularly difficult to control because the ground itself may continue burning beneath the surface. Dry conditions and strong winds can then spread smoke across a much wider area.

The effects are not limited to cloudy skies. Schools may reduce outdoor activities, flights can be delayed and people with breathing difficulties may experience greater discomfort. Businesses and tourism may also be affected when poor air quality continues for several days.

However, haze is not a problem that one country can solve alone. Governments, companies and communities all have a role to play. Preventing unnecessary fires, protecting peatlands and sharing information between countries can reduce the problem.

The wind may carry smoke across borders, but responsibility must travel across them too.
